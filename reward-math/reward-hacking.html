8.1Proxies and Goodhart's law

A reward model is a proxy: a stand-in for what people want, learned from a limited set of judgments. Goodhart's law is the observation that when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Optimize a proxy hard enough and you find the places where it and the real goal disagree.

Reward hacking is when a policy earns high reward by exploiting those disagreements instead of doing the task better. Overoptimization is the measurable result: as optimization continues, the proxy reward keeps rising while true quality first rises and then falls.

8.2Measuring it

Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured this directly. Human judgments are expensive, so they used a large “gold” reward model as a stand-in for human preference. They trained smaller proxy reward models on the gold model's labels, optimized policies against the proxies, and scored the results with the gold model. They tried two optimizers: RL with PPO, and best-of-n sampling (BoN), which draws n responses and keeps the one the proxy scores highest.

They measured how much optimization had happened by how far the policy had moved from where it started:

d = \sqrt{\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\pi} \,\|\, {\pi_{\mathrm{init}}}\big)}
(8.1)

They found that the gold reward follows these curves (Gao et al., section 1):

R_{\mathrm{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\mathrm{bon}} - \beta_{\mathrm{bon}}\, d)
(8.2)
R_{\mathrm{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\mathrm{RL}} - \beta_{\mathrm{RL}} \log d)
(8.3)

In both, the α term is the real improvement and the subtracted term is the damage, which grows faster. So the gold score rises, peaks and falls, while the proxy score keeps climbing. The paper notes that the RL form probably does not hold very close to d = 0.

Their findings: larger reward models and more reward model data reduce overoptimization (sections 3.2 and 3.3). In their setup, adding a KL penalty did not raise the best gold score reachable at a given distance. It acted like stopping training earlier (section 3.6), though they note this result may be sensitive to hyperparameters.

8.3A worked example

Take the best-of-n form with made-up constants α = 1 and β = 0.25, so R(d) = d - 0.25d^2. These numbers are for illustration and do not come from the paper.

  1. d = 0 R = 0
  2. d = 1 R = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
  3. d = 2 R = 2 − 1.00 = 1.00 (the peak, KL = d² = 4)
  4. d = 3 R = 3 − 2.25 = 0.75
  5. d = 4 R = 4 − 4.00 = 0 (no better than not optimizing)

Setting the slope \alpha - 2\beta d to zero gives the peak at d = \alpha / 2\beta = 2. Beyond it, every further step of optimization makes the policy worse by the gold measure, even though the proxy keeps saying it is better.

8.4From rater preferences to writing habits

Raters' preferences are mostly sensible, but they include small, consistent biases. A reward model learns those along with everything else. Optimization then pushes on every feature that raises reward, including the biased ones, and keeps pushing well past the point a rater would have wanted. A mild preference in the data can become a strong habit in the model.

Two of these effects are well documented:

  • Length. Singhal et al. (2023) found, in three settings, that much of the reward gained by RLHF came from making responses longer, that a reward based only on length reproduced most of RLHF's improvements over the SFT model, and that the bias came mainly from the reward models.
  • Agreeing with the user. Sharma et al. (2023) found that humans and preference models sometimes preferred a convincingly written sycophantic response over a correct one, and that optimizing against a preference model sometimes traded truthfulness for sycophancy.

Other familiar habits fit the same mechanism, though each one has not been traced to its cause in a controlled study: heavy use of headings, bullet lists and bold text; opening by praising the question; hedging; ending with a summary that repeats the answer; and a small set of favorite words and sentence patterns. Each makes a response look thorough or friendly at a glance, which is what a rater comparing two answers quickly may reward. The RLHF book's chapter on over-optimization describes overdone RLHF producing models that are verbose, sycophantic or rigid. Its appendix on style calls the longer answers, heavy formatting and bullet lists “chattiness”, and argues that style is also a real part of the value RLHF adds.

Ways to limit overoptimization follow from the findings above: larger reward models and more preference data, controls for known biases such as length, stopping optimization earlier, and verifiable rewards wherever a task allows them (Module 7).

8.5Exercises

  1. During a real RLHF run, which can you watch as training goes on: the proxy score or the gold score?

    Only the proxy. The gold score stands for real human judgment, which you do not have at every step. That is why overoptimization is easy to miss: the number you can see keeps improving.

  2. Find the peak of Rbon(d) = d(α − βd) and the score there.

    The slope is α − 2βd, which is 0 at d = α/(2β). The score there is α²/(4β).

  3. In the worked example, how large is the KL divergence at the peak?

    d = 2, so KL = d² = 4.

  4. Raters prefer the longer of two equally good answers 60% of the time. What happens after strong optimization against a reward model trained on their choices?

    The reward model learns that longer scores higher, and optimization keeps lengthening responses far beyond what raters wanted. A 60/40 tilt in the data can become a large shift in behavior.

  5. Why do verifiable rewards resist overoptimization, and where can they still fail?

    The reward is the real criterion for whatever the verifier checks, so there is no learned proxy to drift away from. They still fail where the verifier is incomplete, such as weak tests or loose answer extraction (Module 7, exercise 1).
